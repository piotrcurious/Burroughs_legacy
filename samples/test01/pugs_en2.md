THE DOGS REMEMBERED THE PALACES

A small account of two transmigrations, one impossible colour, and the curious afterlife of political ambition.

On the fourth morning after the change, the white dog began inspecting the locks.

Not the doors. The locks.

He moved from room to room, standing on his short hind legs where possible, putting his nose close to the brass fittings, listening to the faint clicks of the mechanisms. The front door interested him particularly. He spent eleven minutes examining it, then sat down in the corridor and refused to let the black dog pass.

The black dog had other concerns.

He had discovered a patch of sunlight on the kitchen tiles. He stood in it, turned around, examined his sides, and tried to lie down in such a manner that the sunlight would cover his entire body. When the sun moved, he followed it. When a cloud appeared, he barked at the ceiling.

The owner, who had been living with these two animals for seven years, noticed the changes but did not immediately attach any significance to them.

Dogs have their moods. Dogs sometimes decide that yesterday's perfectly acceptable staircase has become an instrument of torture. They can develop extraordinary grievances against harmless objects. One of his previous dogs had once refused to enter a particular room for three months because a balloon had burst there.

But this was different.

The white dog had stopped playing.

The black dog had become convinced that he was somebody else.

The owner first noticed the latter problem during the grooming session.

Winston, the black pug, had always enjoyed being brushed. He would lie on his side and make little grunting noises, occasionally turning over to facilitate access to the more difficult regions.

Now he watched the brush approach with visible suspicion.

He inspected his reflection in the wardrobe mirror. He turned sideways. He turned again. He examined the black fur on his back.

Then he walked to the white dog's basket and attempted to occupy it.

Misha, the white pug, objected.

A brief argument followed, involving teeth, snorting and an overturned slipper.

When it was over, Winston sat in the white dog's basket, looking rather pleased with himself.

The owner removed him.

Winston returned.

The owner removed him again.

Winston barked, apparently surprised that a simple question of personal preference had become a matter of negotiation.

It was then that the owner remembered something he had seen on television the previous evening.

---

The programme had been showing a collection of photographs from Moscow. There was a familiar succession of long corridors, enormous tables, guards, polished floors and men waiting outside doors.

Misha had been sleeping.

At the sight of the Kremlin, he had opened one eye.

At the sight of a particular office, he had raised his head.

When the camera moved towards a familiar desk, he had stood up and approached the television.

The owner had thought this amusing.

Dogs recognise faces, after all. They recognise voices. Perhaps the white pug had simply associated a particular person with the smell of the television, although this seemed unlikely.

But when the programme showed a photograph of a country residence, Misha had begun scratching at the television cabinet.

He scratched for several minutes.

Then he went to the front door.

The owner opened it.

The dog stood on the threshold, looking out into the garden.

He did not want to go outside.

He seemed to be expecting something.

The black dog came up behind him and pushed past.

Misha let him go.

The owner closed the door.

That night Misha slept facing the entrance to the bedroom.

---

Winston's television habits were more theatrical.

He had developed a particular reaction to Donald Trump.

When the familiar face appeared, Winston would begin barking before the sound had been turned up. He barked at the mouth, at the hair, at the people around him, and sometimes at the lower part of the screen, where subtitles appeared.

On one occasion, a photograph of Mar-a-Lago appeared.

Winston leapt off the sofa and ran to the television.

He began pawing at the screen.

Then he ran to the hallway, returned, and pawed at the screen again.

The owner showed him a photograph of the building on a tablet.

Winston licked it.

He then turned the tablet over and sniffed the back.

The owner tried a photograph of another large house.

No reaction.

A photograph of a golf course.

Winston barked.

A photograph of Trump standing on a golf course.

Winston became excited, circled the table twice and attempted to climb onto the owner's lap.

The owner put the tablet away.

Winston spent the next ten minutes looking for it.

The man began to notice a curious distinction between the dogs.

Misha reacted to places.

Winston reacted to possession.

---

The changes had brought with them other peculiarities.

Misha had become an early riser. At five thirty he would walk through the house, inspect every room and return to his bed. He disliked unexpected visitors, especially people who came through the front entrance without first making themselves known. He would watch them for a long time, then follow them around the house at a carefully maintained distance.

He was particularly interested in the man's mobile phone.

He would approach it when it rang, sniff it, and occasionally place one paw on it.

He had no interest in listening.

He seemed to want to know who was calling.

He also disliked the word "perhaps". The owner discovered this by accident when speaking to a friend on the telephone. Whenever he said perhaps, Misha looked up and gave a low growl.

The black pug had developed a fondness for high places.

He tried to climb onto the dining table, the windowsill, the back of the sofa and a small chest of drawers. He was not particularly good at climbing, which made his ambitions rather expensive in terms of broken objects.

He demanded to be lifted.

Once on a raised surface, he would turn around several times before settling down.

He liked to face the room.

He liked people looking at him.

He disliked being photographed from below.

The owner tried to take a picture of him from the floor.

Winston turned his head away.

He tried again.

The dog walked out of the room.

The owner examined the photograph. It was an ordinary picture of a black pug, his small ears folded backwards, his eyes bulging slightly, his mouth open.

There was no apparent reason for his dissatisfaction.

Perhaps he wanted a better angle.

Perhaps he remembered a different face.

---

The first book was found accidentally.

The man had gone to the attic looking for a box of old photographs and discovered, beneath a stack of magazines, several books on Buddhism.

He remembered that his uncle had once travelled in India and returned convinced that European ideas about the self were fundamentally mistaken.

The books were old, with yellowing pages and pencil annotations.

He read about rebirth, karma and the continuity of mental tendencies. He read about the five aggregates and the refusal of Buddhist thought to identify an enduring self behind the processes of experience.

There was something uncomfortable about this.

He had always imagined reincarnation as a relatively straightforward exchange: one body dies, another receives the soul.

But the texts described something less like the movement of a passenger between houses and more like a succession of conditions, each generating the next.

A flame lighting another flame.

A river that remains a river without ever containing the same water.

He closed the book.

Misha was lying beside the radiator.

Winston was trying to eat a piece of cardboard.

The man looked at the dogs.

An impossible thought had begun to take shape.

He did not like it.

But the more he resisted it, the more the observations seemed to arrange themselves around it.

He fetched his notebook.

Under the heading Behavioural anomalies, he wrote:

WHITE DOG — PUTIN?

BLACK DOG — TRUMP?

He stared at the question marks.

Then he added:

Possible transfer of karmic tendencies.

The following morning, he showed Misha a photograph of Vladimir Putin.

The dog approached it, sniffed the paper and sat down.

He showed Winston a photograph of Donald Trump.

The black dog began barking.

The owner turned the pages around.

The reaction remained.

He tried a photograph of a different politician.

Nothing.

He tried another.

Nothing.

He tried photographs of the two men in different clothes, at different ages, in different locations.

The dogs recognised them.

He was becoming frightened.

---

There was a problem, however.

Both men were alive.

The television showed them moving through their days, speaking, receiving visitors, attending meetings. Their bodies appeared perfectly serviceable.

The man considered several possibilities.

Perhaps the original personalities had been duplicated.

Perhaps the dogs were merely imitating the television.

Perhaps he had become excessively sensitive to ordinary canine behaviour.

He even considered the possibility that the whole thing was a form of mental contagion: having invented the hypothesis, he was now arranging every subsequent observation to confirm it.

This last possibility seemed particularly reasonable.

He decided to investigate it before accepting the more extraordinary explanation.

He spent several afternoons at the municipal library, reading about Buddhism and personal identity.

One day he encountered a monk in the history section.

The monk was looking at a large atlas.

The man explained his problem.

The monk listened, then asked him to describe the two dogs.

The man did so.

"Are they eating?"

"Yes."

"Sleeping?"

"Yes."

"Defecating?"

"Unfortunately."

The monk nodded.

"Then they are alive."

"But I think they contain the souls of two men who are also alive."

The monk closed the atlas.

"Why is that a problem?"

The man tried to explain.

The monk seemed unconcerned.

"Perhaps you misunderstand the word alive."

He took a book from the shelf and opened it at a marked page.

The passage discussed the relationship between the body and the aggregates, the impermanence of conditioned things and the error of treating a continuity of processes as proof of an unchanging self.

The monk read it silently, then passed it to the man.

"This does not say what you think it says," he remarked. "But perhaps it will help you ask a different question."

"What question?"

The monk looked towards the newspapers.

"Whether the person is still there."

---

He returned after lunch.

This time he brought a suitcase.

Inside were newspapers, magazines, printed transcripts, photographs and several folders. The monk opened them across a reading table.

The man began looking through the collection.

There were hundreds of pages.

The first folder concerned political language.

It contained examples of statements repeated almost verbatim over years, sometimes by the same people, sometimes by their successors.

The second concerned public appearances.

Photographs of meetings, ceremonies, televised speeches and official visits. Different locations, different participants, but remarkably similar poses.

The third concerned the disappearance of political memory.

A statement contradicted by another statement. An event described differently by two officials. A promise made, forgotten, revived and presented as a new discovery.

The monk watched the man read.

"Do you see something?"

The man looked up.

"I see people repeating themselves."

"Yes."

"But that doesn't mean they have lost their souls."

The monk shrugged.

"Perhaps not. But look at the pictures."

The man returned to the photographs.

There was something strange about the faces.

It was not that they were expressionless. They were often animated, sometimes smiling, sometimes angry. But their expressions seemed strangely detached from the situations in which they appeared.

They performed the gestures associated with their positions.

They stood in the right places.

They looked towards the cameras.

They received the flowers.

They signed the documents.

They spoke.

The monk placed two photographs beside one another.

In the first, a man was welcoming a visiting delegation.

In the second, another man was welcoming another delegation.

The rooms were different.

The gestures were almost identical.

"An old problem," said the monk. "The body can continue to perform a habit after the reason for the habit has vanished."

The man looked at the photographs again.

"And you think the souls have gone?"

"I think you should look at your dogs."

---

The journey home was strangely quiet.

Misha sat in the passenger seat, watching the landscape pass.

Winston slept.

At one point they stopped at a petrol station. A television was showing a news report.

Winston woke up.

He looked towards the screen.

There was Donald Trump.

The dog stood up, placed his front paws against the dashboard and began barking.

The man turned the volume down.

Winston continued barking.

The screen changed.

A sports programme appeared.

Winston sat down.

He watched a few seconds of football.

Then he went back to sleep.

The man drove on.

He began thinking about the monk's photographs. Perhaps the strange thing was not that people appeared to be behaving like machines. Perhaps the strange thing was that nobody expected anything else.

The dog in the passenger seat had no idea of these considerations.

He had discovered a small smell coming from the glove compartment.

He was investigating it with great determination.

---

Over the next several weeks the man began conducting more elaborate tests.

He found photographs of places associated with the dogs' apparent former lives.

Misha reacted to the Kremlin, the Novo-Ogaryovo residence and several locations in Moscow.

He also reacted to photographs of wooded grounds and formal interiors.

Winston reacted to Mar-a-Lago, the golf courses and several recognisable rooms.

He appeared particularly interested in a photograph of a large golden entrance.

When the man showed him a picture of the White House, he seemed less impressed.

He sniffed it and turned away.

The man tried a photograph of a hotel ballroom.

Winston barked.

He tried a picture of a shopping centre.

Nothing.

He tried another picture of Mar-a-Lago.

Winston barked again, then went to the kitchen and knocked over a bag of dog biscuits.

The man decided that the black pug's memory was selective.

Perhaps he remembered the places where he had been admired.

Misha's memory was different.

He seemed to remember where he had been obeyed.

One afternoon, the owner moved a chair in the living room.

Misha immediately began barking.

The man moved it back.

The dog stopped.

He moved it again.

Misha barked again.

The man put the chair in a completely different place.

Misha examined the room, walked around the chair and went to sleep.

Perhaps the old man had spent too much of his previous life arranging furniture.

---

The dogs did not understand their reincarnation.

They did not know that they had been presidents.

They did not know that they had lost their former bodies, their assistants, their motorcades or their enormous collections of expensive objects.

They knew the smell of the garden.

They knew the sound of the refrigerator opening.

They knew the location of the biscuits.

They knew which chair was most comfortable.

Winston knew that being lifted onto a table was better than climbing onto it himself.

Misha knew that nobody could enter the bedroom without passing the door where he slept.

They had become accustomed to their new circumstances.

This was perhaps the most disturbing part.

The man had expected some sign of suffering, some manifestation of a memory that could not be reconciled with the present.

Instead, they adjusted.

The former President of Russia had discovered that the house contained a warm radiator.

The former President of the United States had discovered that the owner was willing to share his sandwiches.

Both had become increasingly proficient at the business of being dogs.

---

One afternoon, the man returned to the library.

The monk was absent.

He found a note on the table, written in a small hand.

It contained a reference to a passage in the Dhammapada concerning the mind and its conditioning.

The man looked it up.

He did not find an answer to his question.

Instead, he found another question, then another, and another.

He was beginning to suspect that the entire business of reincarnation was being misunderstood.

Perhaps there was no fixed identity to transfer.

Perhaps the dogs had inherited patterns of attachment, aversion and expectation, while the apparent owners continued their biological existence as the remaining collection of habits.

Perhaps what had moved was not a soul but a particular organisation of desire.

He thought about this on the way home.

At the house, Misha was sitting on the stairs.

Winston was trying to get through the gap between the sofa and the wall.

The man watched them.

For a moment he wondered whether the animals had somehow become more human than their former hosts.

Then Winston got stuck.

He began barking.

Misha watched him.

The man helped the black pug out.

Winston immediately tried to get back into the gap.

---

The following morning, a strange thing happened.

The owner was showing the dogs a video of their former hosts.

Putin appeared first.

Misha approached the screen and remained still.

Then the video changed to Trump.

Winston barked.

The owner switched back to Putin.

Misha approached.

He switched to Trump.

Winston barked again.

The two dogs had become very attentive.

The owner let the video run.

Putin spoke.

Trump spoke.

They appeared together in a sequence of interviews, photographs and ceremonial meetings.

The dogs watched.

Then the camera showed a long corridor in a large building.

Misha stood up.

He walked to the television.

He sniffed the screen.

He turned around and looked at the owner.

Winston suddenly jumped onto the sofa.

He placed one paw on the cushion beside Misha.

Misha growled.

Winston growled back.

The owner separated them.

He thought about the photographs in the library, the empty expressions, the repeated gestures, the strange persistence of political routines.

The monk had suggested that the old personalities were gone.

But perhaps they had not been gone at all.

Perhaps they were still somewhere in the world, dispersed among millions of habitual actions, waiting for the right circumstances to become visible again.

Perhaps the dog was not the final destination.

Perhaps the dog was merely a particularly inconvenient place to continue.

---

That evening Misha brought a tennis ball to the owner.

The man was reading.

He looked down.

The white pug had placed the ball beside his foot.

It was the first time he had done so since the change.

The man picked it up.

He threw it gently across the room.

Misha watched it travel.

For a moment the man thought the old personality might be returning.

Then Misha walked to the ball, sniffed it, picked it up and carried it beneath the dining table.

He lay down with the ball between his paws.

He did not play.

He simply kept it.

Winston approached.

He tried to take it.

Misha growled.

Winston barked.

The man went to the kitchen.

He heard the television in the other room.

A familiar voice was announcing something.

The black dog began barking.

The white dog did not move.

The man opened a cupboard and found an old photograph of the two pugs taken before the change.

They were sitting together in the garden.

Misha was smiling, tongue out, eyes half closed.

Winston was looking directly at the camera.

He placed the photograph on the table.

Then he turned off the television.

For a while there was no sound except the dogs breathing.

He looked at the photograph again.

Two animals.

Two expressions.

Two lives.

He wondered whether he had ever really known them.

Perhaps the dogs had always contained other people.

Perhaps the people had always contained dogs.

Perhaps identity was not something one possessed but something that temporarily occupied a particular arrangement of bones, memories, expectations and appetites.

Winston began snoring.

Misha stood up, walked to the door and waited.

The man put on his shoes.

Outside, the evening was cold.

They went for a walk.

At the end of the street, Misha stopped beside a lamppost and examined it with great concentration. Winston discovered a discarded piece of bread.

The man waited.

Two former rulers of the world were standing in the street, one inspecting a municipal fixture, the other eating something that had fallen from a shopping bag.

A bus went past.

Nobody recognised them.

For the first time in their respective careers, this seemed to suit them perfectly.
