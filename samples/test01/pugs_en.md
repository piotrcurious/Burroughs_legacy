THE LAST REINCARNATION OF TWO VERY IMPORTANT MEN

A little investigation into the continuity of the soul, the instability of political identity, and the digestive habits of enlightened beings.

The trouble began with a sausage.

It was a Wednesday morning, although there was nothing particularly Wednesday-like about it. The man had bought sausages the previous evening, put them in the refrigerator, forgotten about them, and was now attempting to make breakfast while two pugs conducted their usual negotiations around his ankles.

The white one, Misha, had always been a friendly animal. A little foolish, perhaps, but generous in spirit. He would bring visitors a toy, even if the visitors had come to repair the plumbing. He enjoyed chasing balls, greeting children and falling asleep in the middle of activities that he had initiated himself.

The black one, Winston, was more suspicious. He had a complicated relationship with the outside world, particularly with bicycles, umbrellas and people wearing hats. He was nevertheless a good companion, with a strong sense of humour and a remarkable ability to locate food concealed in packaging that human beings had considered secure.

This morning, however, Misha was standing in front of the refrigerator, blocking access.

Winston was eating his breakfast.

Both were doing something they had never done before.

The man tried to move Misha.

Misha resisted.

He did not bite. He simply stood there, a small white monument to an unknown principle of territorial sovereignty.

"Move."

Misha looked at him.

"Come on. Move."

The dog turned his head towards the kitchen window.

Winston had meanwhile finished his food and was attempting to eat Misha's.

Normally, Misha would have protested.

Today he did not even look.

The man picked up Winston and put him on the floor.

Winston immediately returned to the bowl.

The man picked him up again.

This time Winston barked.

It was not an ordinary bark. It was an indignant, rather theatrical bark, the sort of noise made by someone who has discovered that the world has failed to recognise his importance.

The man looked at the two animals.

Something had happened.

He was not yet prepared to call it supernatural.

He had lived long enough to know that the supernatural frequently turned out to be a plumbing problem.

---

By lunchtime he had discovered several further irregularities.

Misha had taken possession of the living room. He sat on the largest cushion, facing the entrance, and regarded the other furniture as territory that might eventually be annexed.

He was no longer interested in toys.

The man rolled a tennis ball towards him.

Misha watched it roll.

The ball hit his paw.

He looked down.

The man waited.

Misha lifted his paw, moved the ball aside and returned to watching the entrance.

The man threw the ball again.

Misha sighed.

He actually sighed.

It was an extraordinary performance for an animal that had previously considered the arrival of a tennis ball sufficient justification for abandoning all other activities.

Meanwhile Winston had discovered a photograph of himself.

The photograph showed him sitting on the garden bench, looking reasonably attractive in the afternoon sunlight.

He sniffed it.

Then he looked at his actual reflection in the glass door.

He growled.

The man moved the photograph.

Winston followed it.

He moved it again.

Winston followed it again.

There was something distinctly unpleasant about the expression on the dog's face. It was as if he had discovered that his passport photograph had been taken by a hostile intelligence service.

The man put the photograph away.

Winston barked at the cupboard.

The man gave him a biscuit.

Winston ate it.

The matter appeared settled.

But it was not.

---

Over the following days the differences became impossible to ignore.

Misha had developed an extraordinary attachment to television news.

He did not watch television continuously. He showed no interest in cartoons, nature programmes or football. But when a particular Russian face appeared, he would approach the screen and sit down.

The first time this happened, the man was watching an evening report about negotiations between several countries.

A man appeared on screen, standing beside a large desk.

Misha stopped scratching himself.

His ears moved.

The dog approached the television and sat.

The man changed the channel.

Misha looked at him.

The man returned to the previous channel.

Misha resumed watching.

There was a close-up of the man's face.

Misha made a noise that the owner had previously heard only when a large dog passed the garden gate.

"Don't tell me," the man said. "You know him?"

Winston entered the room.

The screen changed to a photograph of Donald Trump.

Winston became hysterical.

He jumped at the television, barked, ran around the coffee table and attempted to climb onto the window sill.

Then, quite suddenly, he stopped.

He looked down at his own paws.

The man followed his gaze.

Four black feet.

Winston looked at the television again.

He barked twice.

Then he walked into the hallway and refused to come back.

The man began to wonder whether he had accidentally acquired two political refugees.

---

He started an investigation.

This involved taking photographs from newspapers, searching the internet and making notes about the dogs' reactions.

The results were consistent.

Misha recognised the Kremlin.

He recognised several official buildings in Moscow.

He recognised photographs of Putin's country residences, although his reaction to a particular palace was especially interesting.

He approached the picture, sniffed it, then sat with his back perfectly straight.

He remained there for twenty minutes.

The man offered him a biscuit.

No response.

He offered another.

Nothing.

Then Winston entered the room.

Misha growled.

Winston retreated.

The man wrote in his notebook:

White pug. Apparently remembers property. Strong territorial instincts. No longer interested in entertainment. May have developed an administrative position.

Winston was more complicated.

He recognised Mar-a-Lago.

He recognised the golf courses.

He recognised photographs of large golden rooms and people standing beside brightly coloured buildings.

His reaction to these was ecstatic.

He wagged his tail, barked, ran in circles and then attempted to mount the television cabinet.

However, when shown photographs of Donald Trump beside black vehicles, dark suits and black leather furniture, his mood deteriorated.

The man began to suspect that Winston had an objection to his present colour.

He tested the theory with a black towel.

Winston barked at it.

He tried a white towel.

Winston ignored it.

He tried a photograph of a white dog.

Winston approached it and sniffed.

He then turned around and inspected his own back.

The man tried not to laugh.

Winston looked at him.

It was the look of an animal that had been promised a completely different life and had subsequently discovered the small print.

---

There was a book in the house about Buddhism.

It had belonged to the man's father, who had acquired it during a brief period of interest in meditation, after which he had returned to drinking coffee, complaining about politicians and buying unnecessary electrical equipment.

The man had never paid much attention to it.

Now he read it.

There was something about the cycle of existence, about the movement of consciousness through successive forms, about the strange persistence of tendencies and attachments.

He read about karma.

He read about the absence of a permanent self.

He read that a being might continue through successive existences without carrying precisely the same identity from one body to another.

He found this difficult to reconcile with his observations.

Misha was unmistakably Misha in body, but something else seemed to have arrived.

Winston was unmistakably Winston, except that he now behaved like a person who had lost everything except his appetite and his resentment.

The man began to think about reincarnation.

He knew the conventional story: a soul leaves a dead body and enters another.

But what if the body did not die?

What if the soul simply departed?

He looked at the television.

Putin was speaking.

Misha was sitting beside the screen.

The man looked at Winston, who was attempting to remove a piece of dried grass from between his toes.

The answer was becoming increasingly obvious.

He wrote two names on a piece of paper.

PUTIN.

TRUMP.

Then, underneath:

WHITE.

BLACK.

He looked at the paper for a long time.

There was a curious symmetry to it.

The former Russian leader was now white.

The former American leader was now black.

Perhaps samsara had a sense of humour.

---

The problem was that the original hosts were still alive.

The man checked the news.

Both appeared regularly. Both spoke. Both issued instructions. Both appeared at public events. Both seemed capable of performing their respective duties.

The reincarnation hypothesis therefore required an additional mechanism.

He returned to the library.

It was a quiet institution, with carpets that absorbed footsteps and heating that made everyone feel slightly older than they were.

He brought both dogs.

Misha immediately selected a position beside the philosophy section.

Winston began sniffing the bags of other visitors.

The librarian looked at the dogs, then at the man.

"Are they allowed?"

"Educational research," he said.

She accepted this.

He spent the morning reading about consciousness, the Buddhist concept of anatta, the relationship between mind and body, and the problem of personal identity.

He found a number of passages that seemed relevant, but none that directly answered the question.

Could a living person become an empty shell?

Could the body continue while the person had somehow departed?

Was there a distinction between being biologically alive and being spiritually present?

He was taking notes when someone sat down opposite him.

The stranger wore a simple robe and had the sort of face that made it difficult to determine his age. He had been reading a newspaper.

"You seem troubled," he said.

"Possibly."

The man explained.

He told him about the dogs, the photographs, the television, the former residences and the extraordinary personality changes.

He described the white pug's refusal to play.

He described the black pug's dissatisfaction with his pigmentation.

He explained that the two original men were apparently still alive.

The monk listened.

When the man finished, the monk shrugged.

"Why should that matter?"

"Because they are alive."

The monk put down his newspaper.

"Does being alive mean that someone is there?"

The man did not immediately answer.

The monk opened his book and pointed to a passage.

It was a passage concerning the aggregates, attachment and the absence of an enduring self. It did not say that political leaders could literally lose their souls while continuing to breathe. The monk did not seem particularly interested in this distinction.

"Read it," he said.

The man read.

"Again."

He read it again.

The monk nodded.

"Now you understand?"

"No."

"Good."

The monk stood up.

"Wait here."

---

He returned with newspapers.

Lots of newspapers.

They had been collected over several years, from different countries and political traditions. Some were old. Some were relatively recent. A few had been cut out and arranged in folders.

The monk laid them across the table.

"Look carefully."

There were headlines about political promises that had been repeated for decades.

There were photographs of meetings where people appeared to be waiting for instructions.

There were reports of institutions continuing procedures long after their original purposes had become obscure.

There were announcements that contradicted previous announcements.

There were speeches in which the same phrases appeared with the regularity of advertising jingles.

There were official statements that seemed to have been generated by a machine trained exclusively on other official statements.

The monk moved one newspaper towards him.

"Read."

The headline concerned an announcement.

The man read it.

The monk moved another.

"Read."

Another announcement.

A third.

Another announcement about the first announcement.

A fourth.

A response to the third.

A fifth.

A clarification.

A sixth.

A correction of the clarification.

The man began to laugh.

The monk did not.

"They have bodies," the monk said. "They have offices. They have voices. They have schedules. They have people who carry their papers and people who open doors for them. They have meals brought at the correct time. They have photographers."

He tapped the table.

"But where is the person?"

The man looked at the headlines.

The monk continued.

"A man can lose his mind while preserving his habits. An institution can lose its purpose while preserving its procedures. A civilisation can lose its imagination while increasing the number of its announcements."

He pointed to a photograph.

"Look at his face."

The man looked.

It was an ordinary photograph of a political leader at a press conference.

The eyes appeared to be looking in the direction of the camera, but somehow not at anything.

The monk pointed at another photograph.

"And there."

Another face.

Another expression.

Another microphone.

The same peculiar vacancy.

"They have not died," the monk said. "They have simply become available."

"Available for what?"

The monk looked at the two dogs.

"For something else."

---

The man drove home with the newspapers in the boot of the car.

Misha slept.

Winston barked at a cyclist.

At home, the man placed all the newspapers on the dining table.

He turned on the television.

There was a report about international affairs.

Misha entered the room.

He looked at the screen.

Then he looked at the newspapers.

Then he went to his cushion.

Winston came in a minute later.

He watched the television for thirty seconds, became angry about something, and went to the kitchen.

The man sat down.

It was clear now.

The bodies on television were still functioning. They were performing the activities that had once been connected to their identities, but something had departed.

The souls had moved.

Putin was now a white pug.

Trump was now a black pug.

The original hosts had become something like automatic administrative systems, running on accumulated routines and external instructions.

The monk had not said that this was the only possible interpretation.

He had merely shrugged.

But the newspapers were persuasive.

And the dogs were behaving exactly as the man would expect their new identities to behave if those identities had been reduced to their most persistent characteristics.

---

The next morning, Misha refused to go for a walk.

The man put on his shoes.

Misha looked at the leash.

He turned away.

"Come on."

Nothing.

The man attached the leash to his collar.

Misha walked to the door, stopped, and looked at Winston.

Winston was eating something he had found beneath the refrigerator.

"Leave it!"

Winston swallowed.

Misha remained motionless.

The man opened the door.

Misha did not move.

The man gently pulled the leash.

Misha sat down.

It was an extraordinary scene.

The dog had apparently spent an entire previous existence giving instructions to people who had to open doors for him, and now he was confronted with the humiliating reality that he could not even decide when to go outside.

The man waited.

After a few minutes Misha finally stood up and went out.

He walked to the first tree.

He sniffed it.

He urinated.

He sniffed it again.

Then he returned to the house.

The man wondered whether this was the first time in several years that Putin had done something without a security detail.

Winston was enjoying himself.

He ran through the garden, chased a leaf, attempted to catch a fly and barked at the neighbour's cat.

He then fell into a flower bed.

The man picked him up.

Winston licked his face.

For a moment the man thought he saw something resembling happiness.

Then Winston noticed the white dog.

He tried to push him out of the way.

---

The realisation that followed was unexpected.

Perhaps the dogs were not suffering.

Perhaps the reincarnation had been a kind of liberation.

Misha had lost his palace, his offices, his motorcade and the entire apparatus of public authority.

But he had also lost the obligation to pretend to be doing something important every minute of the day.

Winston had lost his golf courses, his private residences, his television appearances and his enormous collection of things bearing his name.

But he had gained the ability to sleep whenever he wanted.

The man began to wonder whether the punishment had been too gentle.

After all, both dogs had warm beds, regular meals and somebody who loved them.

They had no need to understand the world.

They had no need to defend their former careers.

They had no need to construct narratives about their own greatness.

They were simply animals.

There was something almost enviable about this.

---

One evening, while watching the news, the man saw the two former hosts together on television.

They were attending an official meeting.

The camera showed them exchanging greetings, shaking hands and sitting down.

The television commentator described the meeting as historic.

Misha entered the room.

He looked at the screen.

He approached it.

He sniffed.

Then he turned around, walked to the sofa and lay down.

Winston came in.

He saw the television.

He barked.

He ran around the room.

He jumped onto the sofa.

Then he noticed that Misha was occupying the best cushion.

He began pushing him.

Misha growled.

Winston barked.

The man intervened.

"Enough."

They separated.

The news continued.

The man listened to the commentator describe the importance of stability, the need for cooperation and the preservation of existing arrangements.

He looked at the dogs.

The monk's words came back to him.

They have simply become available.

He wondered how many other people were walking around in bodies whose original occupants had quietly departed.

How many offices were occupied by memories of people who no longer existed?

How many institutions were nothing but repeated movements?

How many speeches were being delivered by mouths that had forgotten what it meant to speak?

How many entire systems were continuing because nobody had thought to switch them off?

The television showed a photograph of a large building.

Misha stood up.

He approached the screen.

For a moment the man thought the dog had recognised the place.

But then Misha began scratching his ear.

Perhaps it was nothing.

Perhaps it was simply an itch.

The man decided that not every event required interpretation.

---

At six o'clock the dogs were hungry.

Misha positioned himself by the kitchen cupboard.

Winston stood beside him.

The man opened the food container.

Both animals became attentive.

He poured the food into their bowls.

Misha waited.

Winston began eating immediately.

Misha looked at him.

Winston continued eating.

Misha growled.

Winston moved to the other bowl.

The man watched them.

It was difficult to imagine a more complete collapse of geopolitical grandeur.

Two former heads of state, one of whom had apparently spent an eternity contemplating territorial integrity, the other apparently suffering from a metaphysical disagreement with the colour black, were now engaged in a dispute over a quantity of dry food worth approximately twenty cents.

The man laughed.

Misha looked up.

Winston swallowed.

He placed the bowls in the dishwasher.

The dogs followed him.

Then they went to sleep.

---

That night the man dreamed of a vast empty palace.

There were long corridors, golden ceilings, enormous doors and rooms filled with furniture.

Every room contained a television.

Every television showed a different person speaking.

Nobody listened.

In the largest room, a white dog was sitting on a throne.

He did not move.

A black dog stood beside a mirror, trying to convince it to change colour.

The palace was completely silent except for the sound of a refrigerator.

When the man woke up, both dogs were sleeping at the foot of his bed.

He looked at them.

He thought about the monk.

He thought about the newspapers.

He thought about the Buddhist doctrine of impermanence, the strange continuity of habit, the possibility that the self might be less substantial than its accumulated desires.

Then he noticed that Winston had urinated on the carpet.

Misha was looking at the stain.

The man got up.

He fetched a cloth.

He cleaned the carpet.

The dogs watched.

For a moment he considered whether he himself might be the reincarnation of somebody else.

Perhaps a person who had once occupied a much more important body and had now been reduced to a man who cleaned up after two pugs.

Perhaps the whole universe was doing something similar.

Perhaps everyone was carrying around a little piece of an earlier existence, mistaking old habits for identity, old possessions for destiny, old political territories for the self.

Perhaps the point was not to discover who had been transferred into which body.

Perhaps the point was to discover what remained after the transfer.

He looked at Winston.

Winston was licking the carpet.

He looked at Misha.

Misha was asleep.

The answer, apparently, was not very complicated.

Hunger.

Sleep.

Territory.

The desire to be liked.

The inability to play.

And the persistent conviction that the food in somebody else's bowl was better.

The man went downstairs to make coffee.

Behind him, the black pug began barking at his own reflection.

The white pug did not move.

Somewhere on television, another historic announcement was being made.
